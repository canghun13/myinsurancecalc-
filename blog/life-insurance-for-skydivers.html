Life Insurance for Skydivers: Why Carrier Choice Decides Your Price

10 min read · Updated August 2026

Skydiving occupies a strange place in life insurance. It is statistically far safer than its reputation — the United States Parachute Association recorded roughly 3.65 million jumps in 2023 resulting in 10 fatalities, a rate of about 0.27 deaths per 100,000 jumps — and yet it remains the activity most likely to appear by name on an application form.

That mismatch works both ways. Some carriers still treat any jumper as an automatic downgrade. Others have caught up with the data, and at those carriers an experienced jumper with the right credentials can qualify for the best available rate class with no flat extra at all. The spread between those two outcomes, for the identical logbook, is the largest single variable in skydiving underwriting.

The Credential That Changes Everything

The most consequential fact in this whole topic, and the one most jumpers never hear: at select carriers, a skydiver who is a member of a recognised organisation such as the USPA, jumps at regulated drop zones, and meets the carrier's experience criteria can be written at Preferred Plus — the top rate class — with no avocation charge applied.

This is not a discount for being careful in a vague sense. It is a specific underwriting criterion in specific carrier guidelines. Regulated drop zones operate under recognised safety standards, and organisational membership is verifiable evidence that you jump within that system rather than outside it. An underwriter can price that. What they cannot price, and therefore price conservatively, is an applicant who writes "yes" next to skydiving and nothing else.

The corollary is uncomfortable but worth stating plainly: most skydivers who have been quoted heavy flat extras were matched to the wrong carrier, not correctly evaluated. A bad quote is frequently a distribution problem rather than a risk problem.

What Underwriters Ask

A skydiving supplement is usually short. The variables that drive the outcome are:

  • Total logged jumps and years jumping. Experience reads positively here, unlike in some avocations. A jumper with 800 logged jumps over twelve years generally underwrites better than one with 40 jumps over eight months, because accident data concentrates in the low-experience band.
  • Jumps per year. The frequency question. Someone who made one tandem jump on a birthday is not in the same conversation as someone jumping most weekends.
  • Organisational membership and licence level. USPA membership and licence rating (A through D) are direct inputs at carriers that use them.
  • Drop zone type. Regulated, established drop zones versus informal operations.
  • Discipline. Standard sport jumping is the baseline. BASE jumping, wingsuit flying, and competitive canopy piloting are treated as separate and considerably harder activities. BASE in particular is a frequent outright decline.
  • Instructional or professional involvement. Tandem instructors and camera flyers jump at very high frequency, which shifts the analysis toward occupational underwriting.

An important asymmetry: in diving, more activity is generally worse. In skydiving, more experience can be better while more current frequency is worse. Present both numbers — total jumps and annual jumps — rather than one.

What It Costs

Where a flat extra is applied for skydiving, it commonly falls in the $2.50 to $7.50 per $1,000 of coverage per year range, scaling with jump frequency. Here is what that means for a healthy 40-year-old non-smoker buying $500,000 of 20-year term:

One practical note on flat extras: they are frequently written as temporary and can often be removed later if you stop jumping. If you are quoted one, ask directly whether it is temporary or permanent, and what evidence the carrier would need to remove it. Conversely, a surcharge already in force does not fall away automatically just because you quit — you have to ask for a policy review.

The Exclusion Rider Trade-Off

Some carriers will offer a skydiving exclusion rider rather than a flat extra: the policy issues at normal rates but pays nothing if a skydiving accident causes your death. It is cheaper, and for some people it is the right answer.

The question to ask is what the policy is for. If it exists to cover a mortgage and replace income against illness, cancer, and ordinary accidents — which is what actually kills most people, jumpers included — then an exclusion rider buys you real protection at a real saving. If you are buying specifically because you are aware the sport carries risk, an exclusion rider removes precisely the coverage you wanted. Exclusion riders are also generally permanent once added, so it is not a decision you can revisit cheaply.

Disclose It. Always.

Every life application asks about hazardous activities, and skydiving is almost always named explicitly. Leaving it off is the one genuinely catastrophic mistake available in this topic.

Policies carry a contestability period, typically the first two years, during which the insurer can investigate and rescind coverage for material misrepresentation. If you omit skydiving and die in a jump during that window, the claim can be denied and your family receives nothing after years of premiums. Misrepresentation discovered as fraud can be actionable even beyond the contestability window.

The perverse part is that the risk of disclosure is usually small and the risk of non-disclosure is total. Many jumpers who conceal the activity would have been written at Standard, or even Preferred Plus, had they simply answered the question. There is a separate and much more nuanced situation — taking up the sport after the policy is already in force — which we cover in detail in does life insurance cover extreme sports.

Group Coverage and AD&D as Supplements

Employer group life is typically issued without individual avocation underwriting, so it usually covers jumping without any surcharge or question. That makes it genuinely useful, with two caveats: the amount is commonly capped at one or two times salary, which is well short of most families' needs, and it ends when the job does.

Accidental death and dismemberment coverage is worth understanding correctly. AD&D pays only for accidental death, which does describe a skydiving fatality — but many AD&D policies carry their own hazardous-activity exclusions, and skydiving appears on those lists routinely. Read the exclusions before treating it as a substitute. It is a supplement to a properly underwritten term policy, never a replacement for one.

Bottom Line

Skydiving is more insurable than its reputation suggests, and the single biggest determinant of your price is which carrier reviews the file. Get your credentials in order — organisational membership, licence level, regulated drop zone, logged jumps — describe them precisely, and have a broker who handles avocation cases pre-screen before anything is formally submitted. Above all, disclose the activity. A flat extra is an annoyance; a denied claim is a catastrophe.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can skydivers get life insurance?

Yes. Skydivers are insurable, and at select carriers an experienced jumper who is a member of a recognised organisation such as the USPA and jumps at regulated drop zones can qualify for the best available rate class with no avocation surcharge. Other carriers apply flat extras or decline. The variation between carriers for an identical profile is larger than any other factor in skydiving underwriting.

How much extra does life insurance cost if you skydive?

Where a flat extra applies it commonly runs $2.50 to $7.50 per $1,000 of coverage per year, scaling with jump frequency. On a $500,000 policy for a healthy 40-year-old, a $2.50 flat extra means roughly $158 a month versus about $54 at Standard rates. Many jumpers placed with a favourable carrier pay no surcharge at all.

Do I have to tell my life insurance company I skydive?

Yes, when the application asks. Skydiving is almost always named explicitly on hazardous-activity questions. Omitting it is the one genuinely catastrophic mistake in this area: policies carry a contestability period, typically the first two years, during which the insurer can investigate and rescind coverage for material misrepresentation, leaving your family with nothing. The irony is that many jumpers who conceal it would have been written at Standard rates anyway.

Does USPA membership lower life insurance rates?

At carriers that use it as an underwriting criterion, yes. Membership in a recognised organisation combined with jumping at regulated drop zones is verifiable evidence that you operate within an established safety system, and some carriers treat it as a specific criterion for their best rate classes. It has no effect at carriers that do not use it, which is why carrier selection matters so much.

Will my life insurance pay out if I die skydiving?

If you disclosed the activity and the policy carries no skydiving exclusion, yes - the death benefit is paid like any other accidental death. It will not pay if the policy has an exclusion rider naming the activity, or if you failed to disclose skydiving and the insurer rescinds the policy for misrepresentation. Check your policy documents for an exclusion rider specifically.

Can a skydiving flat extra be removed later?

Often, yes. Flat extras are frequently written as temporary and many carriers will remove the charge if you stop jumping, but it does not fall away automatically - you have to request a policy review and provide updated information. Ask at the point of offer whether the flat extra is temporary or permanent and what evidence would be required to remove it.
