Life Insurance After a Stroke or TIA (Mini-Stroke)

8 min read · Updated November 2026

A stroke doesn’t rule out life insurance, but it changes the timing and the price. Most insurers postpone an application until several months to a year after the event, because the risk of a second stroke is highest early on. After that, the outcome depends mostly on three things: whether it was a TIA or a full stroke, whether it left lasting deficits, and whether the cause was found and treated. A single TIA years ago with a clean workup is a very different file from a hemorrhagic stroke last year.

Applying too early produces a postponement, not a permanent decline, but every application is recorded with the MIB (the industry’s shared medical information exchange), so it’s better to apply once you’re past an insurer’s window than to collect postponements.

What Underwriters Look At

  • TIA or stroke, and when. Age at the event matters: a stroke in your 30s or early 40s with no identified cause draws more caution than one at 65 with a clear explanation.
  • First event or recurrence. A second event is far harder to place than a first.
  • Cause. A specific, treated cause is reassuring — for example, atrial fibrillation now on anticoagulation, a closed patent foramen ovale, or a carotid artery treated with surgery or a stent. Unexplained (“cryptogenic”) strokes are rated more cautiously.
  • Residual impairment. Full recovery versus lasting weakness, speech, vision or memory problems.
  • Workup. CT or MRI, carotid ultrasound or Doppler, echocardiogram, and heart rhythm monitoring. A complete, normal workup helps; missing tests often lead to postponement.
  • Risk factors. Blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes, coronary or peripheral artery disease, weight and alcohol use. Smoking after a stroke leads many carriers to decline.
  • Treatment adherence. Steady use of blood pressure, statin and antiplatelet or anticoagulant medication counts in your favor.

What a Table Rating Costs

Most stroke and TIA approvals come with a table rating: a surcharge on the Standard premium, usually 25% per table. Table 2 is 150% of Standard, Table 4 is double, and Table 6 is 2.5 times.

Model your own numbers with the table rating calculator. Because ratings improve with time, a smaller policy now plus a request for reconsideration after two or three event-free years is a common strategy: many insurers will review a table rating on an existing policy with updated medical records.

If You Already Have Coverage, Keep It

A policy bought before the stroke is priced on your health when you applied. Insurers can’t raise the rate or cancel it because of a later stroke, so don’t replace it. Check two features:

  • Conversion privilege. Most term policies, and many employer group policies, let you convert to permanent coverage without new medical questions before a deadline. After a stroke, this is often the cheapest way to get lifelong coverage.
  • Living benefits. Some policies include a critical or chronic illness rider that can pay part of the death benefit early after a qualifying stroke. Standalone critical illness insurance bought before a stroke works the same way; it generally can’t be bought after one.

Before You Apply: Records to Gather

  1. Hospital discharge summary and the neurologist’s notes, including the diagnosis (TIA, ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke) and date.
  2. Imaging and test results: CT or MRI, carotid ultrasound, echocardiogram, rhythm monitoring.
  3. The identified cause and what was done about it.
  4. Current medications and doses, and recent blood pressure and cholesterol readings.
  5. A note on recovery: any remaining symptoms, return to work and driving.

An independent broker can send this to several underwriters informally before a formal application, which shows which insurer is likely to give the best rating. Related conditions are covered in our guides to heart disease, high blood pressure and diabetes.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you get life insurance after a stroke?

Yes. Most insurers postpone applications for about 6 to 12 months after an ischemic stroke and around 2 years after a hemorrhagic stroke, then offer coverage with a table rating if recovery is good and risk factors are controlled. Strokes with lasting impairment often lead to simplified or guaranteed issue coverage instead.

How long after a TIA can I get life insurance?

Many insurers will consider an application about 3 to 6 months after a TIA once testing is complete. Table 2 to 3 offers are common, and a single TIA several years ago with no recurrence and well-controlled blood pressure and cholesterol can sometimes qualify for Standard rates.

How much more does life insurance cost after a stroke?

Most approvals carry a table rating of about 25% per table on top of the Standard premium. A Table 4 rating doubles the Standard premium and Table 6 costs 2.5 times Standard. Ratings tend to improve the longer you go without another event.

Will my existing life insurance cover me after a stroke?

Yes. A policy issued before the stroke can't be cancelled or repriced because of it as long as premiums are paid. Many term and group policies also let you convert to permanent coverage without new medical questions before a deadline.

Does accidental death insurance pay for a stroke?

Generally no. A stroke is an illness, not an accident, so accidental death and dismemberment policies don't pay for a death caused by one. Life insurance, including simplified and guaranteed issue policies, does.
