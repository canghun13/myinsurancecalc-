Certificate of Insurance for Your Move: What the Building Wants and Why It Gets Rejected

7 min read · Updated October 2026

Many apartment buildings, co-ops and condos — and almost every managed high-rise in New York City — won’t reserve a freight elevator or let a moving crew through the door until management has approved a certificate of insurance (COI) from the moving company. The requirement comes from the building, not the city, so two buildings on the same block can ask for different things. Get it wrong and the move is rescheduled on the morning it was supposed to happen.

The COI protects the building: if a mover gouges the lobby, damages an elevator or injures someone, the building wants proof that the mover’s insurer pays. It does nothing for your sofa. Both halves are covered below.

What the Building Usually Asks For

Ranges are commonly seen requirements, not a standard. Your building’s written requirements or sample COI control.

How to Get It Approved the First Time

  1. Ask management for the requirements in writing, ideally a sample COI with the names filled in, as soon as you have a move date.
  2. Send the sample to the mover when you book, not the week of the move. A routine certificate can be issued the same day; one with added insureds or endorsements can take several business days, and weekends and holidays slow everything down.
  3. Have the mover send the COI directly to management and copy you, then ask management to confirm approval in writing.
  4. Reserve the elevator only after approval. Many buildings won’t book the elevator or confirm the move window without an approved COI, and some also require a refundable damage deposit or a move fee.
  5. Repeat for both buildings. The building you’re leaving and the one you’re moving into each have their own requirements.

A standard COI should be free. If a mover charges for a basic certificate, treat it as a warning sign; higher limits or special endorsements a building demands may legitimately show up in the quote.

The Five Reasons Buildings Reject a Mover’s COI

  • Names don’t match. The certificate holder or additional insured is the building’s street address or a shortened name instead of the owner’s legal entity. This is the most common problem.
  • Additional insured box checked, no endorsement. The certificate itself says it confers no rights; buildings that know this want the endorsement page behind it. Our explainer on additional insured vs certificate holder covers why.
  • Limits too low, usually the umbrella.
  • Missing workers’ comp or, in New York, disability coverage — often because the crew are classified as independent contractors.
  • The certificate is from the wrong company. If you booked through a broker or an online marketplace, the COI must come from the moving company that actually shows up, not the booking site.

What the COI Doesn’t Cover: Your Belongings

Damage to your own furniture is handled separately, through the mover’s valuation options and your own insurance:

  • Released value is the free default for interstate moves under federal rules and pays only 60 cents per pound per item. A 10-pound television that’s destroyed is worth $6.
  • Full value protection makes the mover repair, replace, or pay replacement value, subject to its deductible and any declared value. It costs extra and is worth reading closely.
  • Renters or homeowners insurance generally covers belongings away from home against named perils such as fire, theft, or a crash of the moving truck, but not breakage from handling or packing. Check your policy’s off-premises limit and whether your move dates are covered at both addresses. Our renters insurance calculator helps size coverage for the new place.

Self-Moves, Labor-Only Crews and Deliveries

A DIY move with a rental truck and friends usually doesn’t need a COI, but many managed buildings require a self-move waiver, a damage deposit, or both — and some don’t allow self-moves through the main elevator at all. Labor-only helpers hired through an app are the riskiest category: they may carry no liability coverage of their own, and the building may treat them like a moving company and ask for a COI they can’t produce. Ask before you book. Furniture delivery and appliance installation crews generally need COIs too; parcel carriers don’t.

The same rules apply when contractors renovate a unit after you move in. If you’re a small business that keeps being asked for certificates, see certificate of insurance explained.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my building need a certificate of insurance from my movers?

The building wants proof that the moving company's insurer will pay if the crew damages common areas or the elevator, or injures someone. Requirements are set by each building or management company, and many will not let movers in or reserve the freight elevator until management approves the certificate.

What should a moving company's COI include?

Typically the building owner or managing agent as certificate holder with their exact legal names and address, the same entities named as additional insureds with an endorsement attached, general liability often at $1 million per occurrence and $2 million aggregate, auto liability, workers' compensation, and in larger buildings an umbrella and a waiver of subrogation. The building's written requirements control.

How long does it take to get a COI from movers?

A routine certificate can be issued the same day. One that adds several additional insureds or special endorsements can take several business days. Send the building's sample COI to the mover when you book.

Does the mover's certificate of insurance cover my belongings?

No. The COI covers the mover's liability to the building and others. Your belongings are covered by the valuation option you choose with the mover, such as released value at 60 cents per pound per item for interstate moves or full value protection, and partly by your renters or homeowners policy.

Do I need a COI if I move myself?

Usually not, but many managed buildings require a self-move waiver, a refundable damage deposit, or both, and some restrict self-moves. Ask management before the move date.
