Life Insurance for Dangerous Hobbies: How Avocation Underwriting Works

11 min read · Updated August 2026

If you skydive, dive, climb, race, or fly, you have probably heard that your hobby will wreck your life insurance rates. That is not quite right, and the gap between the myth and the mechanics costs people real money in both directions — some pay flat extras they did not need, and others get a claim denied because they assumed the question on the application did not apply to them.

Insurers call these activities avocations, and they are underwritten separately from your health. A person in perfect health with a clean driving record can still be rated purely on what they do at weekends. But avocation underwriting is also unusually forgiving of detail: the same activity, described precisely, can move from a decline to Preferred Plus. This guide covers how the pricing actually works, what the numbers look like, and where the real decision points are.

How Avocations Are Priced: Three Different Instruments

Underwriters have three tools for a hazardous hobby, and which one you are offered matters more than most applicants realise, because they behave completely differently over the life of the policy.

InstrumentHow it worksBehaviour over timeTypical use
Flat extraFixed dollar amount per $1,000 of coverage per year, added to the base premiumOften temporary — may drop off after a set number of years, and can sometimes be removed on request if you stop the activityThe default tool for avocations, because the risk is accident-driven and not age-driven
Table ratingPercentage surcharge on the Standard premium, roughly +25% per tablePermanent for the policy term; grows in dollar terms as the base premium growsLess common for hobbies alone; more typical when a health issue is also present
Exclusion riderPolicy issues at normal rates, but pays nothing if death is caused by the excluded activityPermanent, and generally cannot be removed laterOffered when the carrier will not price the risk at all

The critical distinction: a flat extra is the good outcome, and an exclusion rider is a trade you should think hard about. A flat extra means you are insured for the thing you actually do. An exclusion rider means you are paying premiums for coverage that specifically does not apply to your most likely cause of accidental death. It can still be the right call — a policy that excludes climbing but pays for cancer, a car accident, or a heart attack is far better than no policy — but it should be a decision, not a default.

What a Flat Extra Actually Costs

Flat extras for avocations generally run between $2.50 and $7.50 per $1,000 of coverage per year. That sounds abstract until you convert it. Here is a healthy 40-year-old non-smoker buying $500,000 of 20-year term:

Rating appliedEst. monthly premiumEst. annual premiumExtra vs Standard
Standard, no avocation rating~$54~$649—
Flat extra $2.50 per $1,000~$158~$1,899+$1,250/yr
Flat extra $5.00 per $1,000~$262~$3,149+$2,500/yr
Flat extra $7.50 per $1,000~$367~$4,399+$3,750/yr
Table 2 rating (no flat extra)~$81~$974+$325/yr

Two things jump out. First, on a mid-size policy a flat extra dwarfs the base premium — at $2.50 per $1,000 the avocation charge alone is roughly double what the insurance itself costs. Second, a Table 2 rating is far cheaper than even the smallest flat extra at this coverage level. If a carrier offers you a choice between the two, do the arithmetic rather than assuming the table rating sounds worse.

Because the flat extra scales with the face amount and the table rating scales with your age-based premium, which one is cheaper flips depending on your age and coverage. Run your own combination through our table rating and flat extra calculator before you accept an offer.

The Four Variables That Decide Your Rating

Across every hazardous hobby, underwriters are asking the same four questions. Knowing them lets you present your case accurately instead of triggering a worst-case assumption.

  • Frequency. The single biggest driver. Someone who dives twice a year on holiday and someone who dives every weekend are different risks, and most guidelines have explicit frequency bands. Occasional participation frequently produces no rating at all.
  • Intensity or depth. Every activity has a threshold where guidelines change — depth for diving, altitude and technicality for climbing, jump type for skydiving, class of competition for racing. Staying inside the recreational band is usually the difference between Standard and rated.
  • Certification and organisational membership. This is the most underused lever. Formal training through a recognised body demonstrably improves outcomes, and at some carriers membership in the governing organisation for the sport is a specific criterion for the best rate classes.
  • Safety context. Solo versus buddy or guided, regulated venue versus informal, supervised versus unsupervised. Underwriters read these as proxies for judgement, and judgement is what they are actually pricing.

Carrier Choice Matters More Than Anything You Can Change

There is no industry-standard avocation table. The same skydiver with the same logbook can be declined at one carrier and offered the best available rate class at another, and that spread is larger than any behavioural change you could make before applying. Some insurers write avocation risk as a specialty; others treat any hazardous hobby as an automatic downgrade.

This is why the sequence of your application matters so much. A formal declination is reported to the Medical Information Bureau (MIB) and stays on file for seven years, and every subsequent application asks whether you have ever been declined. Applying to three carriers hoping one says yes is actively counterproductive here. An independent broker who handles avocation cases can pre-screen your profile informally with several carriers before anything is formally submitted — and you can request your own free MIB disclosure once a year to see what carriers see.

Prepare a short, factual written summary of your activity before you apply: certification level, years of experience, annual frequency, typical and maximum intensity, venue type, whether you go solo, and any incident history. Vague answers get underwritten as worst case. Specific answers get underwritten as what they are.

When the Hobby Is Not the Real Problem

Avocation ratings are additive to everything else. A recreational diver with well-controlled blood pressure is usually a straightforward case; the same diver with untreated sleep apnea, poorly controlled diabetes, or a recent cardiac history is a much harder placement, because the underwriter now has two risks interacting rather than one.

If you have both a hazardous hobby and a health condition, address the health side first where you can. It is the part underwriters weight most heavily, and it is usually the part you have more control over. It is also worth considering whether disability insurance belongs alongside the life cover — for most of these activities a serious non-fatal injury is considerably more likely than death, and disability policies handle avocation risk differently, typically through exclusions rather than extra premium.

Bottom Line

A hazardous hobby is a pricing question, not an eligibility question. Most recreational participants at moderate frequency, properly certified and honestly described, are insurable at or near Standard rates. The people who end up badly served are the ones who either accept the first offer without checking what instrument was applied, or who leave the activity off the application and hand their family a contestable claim. Describe what you do accurately, get pre-screened before you formally apply, and check the arithmetic on any flat extra you are offered.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do dangerous hobbies always increase life insurance rates?

No. Occasional recreational participation with proper certification frequently produces no rating at all. Frequency and intensity are the main drivers - someone who dives or jumps a few times a year is often written at Standard or better, while weekly participation at advanced levels is where flat extras usually appear. Carrier selection also varies the outcome enormously for identical profiles.

What is a flat extra for a hazardous hobby?

A flat extra is a fixed dollar surcharge per $1,000 of coverage per year, added on top of the base premium. For avocations it commonly runs $2.50 to $7.50 per $1,000 annually. On a $500,000 policy, a $2.50 flat extra adds roughly $1,250 a year. Flat extras are often written as temporary and may drop off after a set number of years, so ask whether the one you are quoted is temporary or permanent.

Is a flat extra or a table rating cheaper?

It depends on your age and coverage amount, and the answer flips between profiles. A flat extra scales with the face amount, while a table rating scales with your age-based premium. For a 40-year-old buying $500,000 of 20-year term, a Table 2 rating adds roughly $325 a year while the smallest typical flat extra adds about $1,250 - so the table rating is far cheaper at that profile. At older ages or smaller face amounts the comparison can reverse.

Should I accept an exclusion rider for my hobby?

It depends on what the policy is for. An exclusion rider means the policy pays nothing if the excluded activity causes your death, while still covering every other cause. If the coverage exists mainly to protect a mortgage or replace income against illness and ordinary accidents, an exclusion rider is usually better than no policy. If the whole reason you are buying is the risk of the activity itself, it defeats the purpose. Exclusion riders are generally permanent and cannot be removed later.

Which carriers are best for hazardous hobbies?

There is no single answer, because guidelines vary by activity and are revised periodically. Some carriers write avocation risk as a specialty while others downgrade any hazardous hobby automatically. The practical approach is to have an independent broker who handles avocation cases pre-screen your profile informally with several carriers before submitting formally, since a formal declination is reported to the MIB and stays on file for seven years.
