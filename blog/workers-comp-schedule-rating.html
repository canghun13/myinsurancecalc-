Workers’ Comp Schedule Rating: How Credits and Debits Are Calculated

8 min read · Updated October 2026

Two contractors with the same payroll, the same class codes and the same experience mod can get workers’ comp quotes thousands of dollars apart. The difference is often a single line on the rating page: the schedule rating modification. It’s the insurer’s judgment of risk features that the class rate and your EMR don’t capture, expressed as a credit or a debit — and unlike your EMR, it’s partly negotiable.

Where Schedule Rating Sits in the Premium Formula

Workers’ comp premium is built in a fixed order. Schedule rating comes right after the experience mod:

  1. Manual premium = payroll ÷ 100 × class rate, for each class code.
  2. × Experience modification (EMR), if you’re experience-rated.
  3. × Schedule rating factor — for example 0.85 for a 15% credit, or 1.10 for a 10% debit.
  4. Then premium discount for larger accounts, the expense constant, and state taxes and assessments.

The North Carolina and Pennsylvania plans both spell out this order: schedule rating is applied multiplicatively after the experience mod and before premium discounts and the expense constant.

That last row is the point: on a mid-sized account, schedule rating can move premium more than a small change in the EMR does. Our EMR calculator shows the experience side; schedule rating is applied on top of it.

The Limit: Usually 25% Either Way

Schedule rating plans are filed with state regulators, and most cap the total at a 25% credit or 25% debit. Pennsylvania’s plan (PCRB) and North Carolina’s plan (NCRB) both set ±25%, as do the NCCI-based worksheets used in states such as South Dakota. How a Schedule Modification Is Calculated

The underwriter goes category by category, assigns a credit, debit or zero within each category’s range, adds them up, and caps the total. A typical worksheet for a contractor with a written safety program and a known claims history might read:

CategoryUnderwriter’s findingCredit (−) / debit (+)
PremisesClean, well-maintained shop and yard−3%
Safety devicesFall protection and machine guarding documented−5%
EmployeesHigh turnover; little documented training+3%
Management cooperationPrompt claim reporting, accurate payroll records−5%
Safety organizationWritten program, monthly meetings, return-to-work program−5%
Total−15% → factor 0.85

Illustrative worksheet.

Regulators require the credits and debits to be backed by evidence. North Carolina’s plan says no credit or debit can take effect before the insurer has supporting evidence in its file and that the insured can ask how the factor was derived; Pennsylvania’s says the carrier must provide the supporting documentation to the insured on request and can’t change the factor mid-term without mutual agreement.

How to Earn a Credit (and Lose a Debit)

  • Send the evidence before the quote. Credits are given for what’s in the underwriting file. A written safety program, training logs, safety meeting minutes, equipment inspection records, a drug-free workplace policy and a return-to-work / light-duty program should go to your agent with the submission, not after.
  • Ask for the worksheet. If your quote shows a debit, ask which categories drove it. Under plans like North Carolina’s, a debit can be removed once you document that the cause has been corrected.
  • Watch it at renewal. Schedule credits are discretionary and move with the market. A credit that disappears at renewal raises premium even when your EMR improves — compare the factor year over year, not just the bottom line.
  • Make the loss-control visit count. The inspector’s report often drives the premises, safety-device and safety-organization categories.
  • Keep payroll and claims reporting clean. “Cooperation with the carrier” covers prompt claim reporting and accurate payroll at audit — the premium audit is part of how you earn it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is schedule rating in workers' comp?

Schedule rating is a credit or debit an insurer applies to workers' comp premium for risk features that the class rate and experience mod don't capture, such as workplace condition, safety equipment, employee training, management cooperation and safety programs. Most state plans cap it at 25% in either direction.

How is a workers' comp schedule modification calculated?

The underwriter assigns a credit or debit within each category's allowed range on a filed worksheet, adds them, and caps the total, usually at plus or minus 25%. The result becomes a factor, such as 0.85 for a 15% credit, applied to premium after the experience modification and before premium discounts and the expense constant.

What is the maximum schedule credit for workers' comp?

Usually 25%. Pennsylvania's and North Carolina's filed plans both cap schedule rating at a 25% credit or 25% debit, and NCCI-based worksheets used in other states commonly do the same. Each category within the plan has its own smaller limit.

Is schedule rating the same as the experience mod?

No. The experience mod is calculated by the rating bureau from your actual claims and payroll and is the same with every insurer. Schedule rating is the insurer's underwriting judgment of your current operation and differs from carrier to carrier.

Can I ask my insurer to remove a schedule debit?

Yes. Ask for the schedule rating worksheet and the reasons for each debit, then document the corrections. Under North Carolina's plan, for example, a debit may be removed once the insurer receives documentation that the cause has been corrected.
