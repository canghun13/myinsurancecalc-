Health Insurance for H-1B, E-2 and Work Visa Holders in 2027

10 min read · Updated October 2026

Until now, a visa holder buying their own coverage was treated much like a citizen on the ACA marketplace: same plans, same income-based premium tax credit. That ends on January 1, 2027. Under the 2025 budget reconciliation law, premium tax credits and cost-sharing reductions are limited to green card holders, Cuban and Haitian entrants, and COFA migrants. H-1B, H-4, L-1, L-2, E-1, E-2, O-1, TN, F-1 and J-1 holders can still enroll — they just pay the full premium.

Who this actually hits is narrower than the headlines suggest. Most H-1B and L-1 employees already get insurance at work. The people who need a new plan are the ones who were buying their own: E-2 treaty investors running small companies, dependents who weren’t on the employer plan, employees of small employers that don’t offer coverage, and people between jobs or between school coverage and a first job. Our status-by-status table covers every category; this guide is about what to do instead.

Employer Coverage Just Got More Valuable

For a family where one spouse is on H-1B and the other is on H-4, the usual question was whether the H-4 spouse and children should join the employer plan or buy a subsidized marketplace plan. With the subsidy gone, the employer plan almost always wins for dependents, because employer premiums come out of pay before tax and the employer pays part of them. If you passed on dependent coverage at the last open enrollment, check whether your employer allows a mid-year change; losing eligibility for a marketplace subsidy is not by itself a special enrollment event for an employer plan, so the next employer open enrollment may be your window.

Large employers — 50 or more full-time-equivalent employees — must offer affordable coverage to full-time staff or pay a penalty, and visa status doesn’t change that. Smaller employers have no such requirement, which is where most uninsured visa holders work.

Buying a Full-Price Marketplace Plan

Lawfully present visa holders can still use HealthCare.gov or their state marketplace. Plans are guaranteed issue, cover pre-existing conditions, and include the ten essential health benefits. Without a subsidy, the strategy changes:

  • Silver loses its edge. Its main advantage was cost-sharing reductions, which visa holders no longer get. Compare gold and bronze on total expected cost — premium plus the deductible you’re likely to hit.
  • Bronze plus an HSA. Since January 1, 2026, every individual-market bronze and catastrophic plan counts as HSA-qualified. Contributions are tax-deductible: up to $4,400 self-only and $8,750 family for 2026, rising to $4,500 and $9,000 for 2027. There is no citizenship requirement for an HSA.
  • Off-exchange is fine. Without a subsidy, there is no penalty for buying the same plan directly from the insurer. Some insurers sell plans off-exchange that aren’t listed on the marketplace.
  • Timing. Open enrollment for 2027 runs November 1, 2026 to January 15, 2027 on HealthCare.gov; enroll by December 15 for a January 1 start.

E-2 Investors: Using the Business

E-2 treaty investors — common among owners from Korea, Japan, Germany, the UK and other treaty countries — usually run small companies and buy their own coverage. Three tools matter more now that the subsidy is gone:

1. A small-group plan

If the company has W-2 employees, it can offer a group plan, and the owner’s family can be on it. Most insurers and states require at least one enrolled employee who isn’t an owner or an owner’s spouse, so a company with only the investor and spouse on payroll usually can’t buy one.

2. An ICHRA (individual coverage HRA)

An ICHRA lets the company reimburse employees, tax-free, for individual-market premiums they buy themselves. For citizens, the catch is that accepting an ICHRA can cost them their marketplace subsidy. For visa holders in 2027 there is no subsidy to lose, which removes the main drawback. Who can participate depends on the company’s tax structure:

Entity typeCan the owner be reimbursed through an ICHRA?
C corporation, owner on W-2 payrollYes — the owner is an employee
S corporation, owner with more than 2%No — use the self-employed deduction instead
Partnership / multi-member LLCNo (partners aren’t employees)
Sole proprietor / single-member LLCNo

An owner’s spouse who is a genuine W-2 employee can sometimes participate and cover the owner as a dependent. Confirm structure questions with a tax professional.

3. The self-employed health insurance deduction

Sole proprietors, partners and more-than-2% S corporation shareholders can deduct premiums for themselves, a spouse and dependents above the line on Form 7206, up to the business’s net profit, in any month they weren’t eligible for an employer-subsidized plan (including a spouse’s). For S corporation owners, the premiums need to be paid or reimbursed by the company and included in W-2 wages to qualify. At a combined federal and state rate of 30%, a $1,200 monthly family premium costs about $840 after tax — not a subsidy, but a meaningful offset. Students and Exchange Visitors

F-1 students are usually required by their school to carry the school plan or an approved equivalent. The gap to watch is the end of the school plan — graduation, or the start of OPT or STEM OPT — when losing that coverage triggers a 60-day special enrollment period for a marketplace plan (at full price from 2027).

J-1 exchange visitors and their J-2 dependents must hold insurance for the whole program that meets federal minimums under 22 CFR 62.14:

Plans That Look Cheaper — and Why to Be Careful

Visitor and “international” medical plans, short-term health plans and health care sharing ministries will all quote far less than a full-price ACA plan. They are not ACA-compliant: they can exclude pre-existing conditions, cap benefits, and leave out maternity or mental health care, and a sharing ministry isn’t insurance at all. Short-term plan length is also limited by federal and state rules. For someone in good health bridging a few weeks, they can be a stopgap; as the main coverage for a family living and working in the U.S., the trade-off is large.

Planning Around a Green Card

If you’re in the green card process, the day your permanent residence is approved is the day you’re back in the eligible group. Keep a marketplace plan in force if you have one, and report the approval immediately: becoming newly eligible for financial help generally lets the advance credit start the following month and opens a window to switch plans. A pending I-485 or an employment authorization document doesn’t count — only approved permanent residence does.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can H-1B visa holders buy health insurance on the marketplace?

Yes. H-1B holders are lawfully present and can enroll in marketplace plans during open enrollment or a special enrollment period. From January 1, 2027, they pay the full premium because work visa holders are no longer eligible for premium tax credits or cost-sharing reductions.

Can E-2 visa holders get health insurance?

Yes. E-2 investors can buy marketplace or off-exchange individual plans at full price, join a company group plan if the business has eligible non-owner employees, use an ICHRA if the company is a C corporation with the owner on payroll, or deduct premiums with the self-employed health insurance deduction if they own an S corporation, partnership or sole proprietorship.

Should my H-4 spouse join my employer plan instead of the marketplace?

In most cases, yes, starting in 2027. Without the premium tax credit, a full-price marketplace plan usually costs more than adding a dependent to an employer plan, whose premiums are paid pre-tax and partly by the employer. Check when your employer allows changes; the next employer open enrollment may be the window.

Can visa holders open a health savings account?

Yes. HSA eligibility depends on having qualifying high-deductible coverage and no other disqualifying coverage, not on citizenship. Since 2026, all individual-market bronze and catastrophic plans are HSA-qualified. The 2027 limits are $4,500 for self-only and $9,000 for family coverage.

What insurance does a J-1 visa require?

Under 22 CFR 62.14, J-1 exchange visitors and their dependents need at least $100,000 of medical benefits per accident or illness, $25,000 for repatriation of remains, $50,000 for medical evacuation, and a deductible of no more than $500 per accident or illness, from an insurer rated A- or better by A.M. Best or an equivalent rating.
