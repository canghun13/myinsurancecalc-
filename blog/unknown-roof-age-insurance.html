“Unknown Roof Age” on Your Home Insurance: How Insurers Fill It In and How to Prove the Real Date

8 min read · Updated October 2026

Roof age is one of the few fields on a homeowners application that can change your premium, your claim payout, and whether you get renewed — and it’s often the one nobody can answer. If you bought the house with an existing roof, or the roofer is long gone, the field gets filled in some other way. Usually the insurer’s way is not in your favor.

How Insurers Fill In the Roof Age

SourceHow it’s usedWeakness
Year the house was builtCommon default when the age is unknownTreats a replaced roof as original — can make a 6-year-old roof look 30
Your applicationSelf-reported dateGuesses at purchase get carried forward for years
Building permit dataPermit records show a reroof dateMany reroofs are never permitted; partial repairs can look like replacements
Aerial and satellite imageryImagery vendors estimate condition and changes over timeCan misread stains, shadows or tree cover; may miss a replacement
InspectionInspector’s visual estimate or a formal roof reportVisual age estimates can be off by years

Data vendors themselves describe roof age as a blind spot: Verisk lists year of construction, permits, imagery and inspector estimates as the main sources and says traditional methods can “fall well short” of a roof’s actual age.

Published underwriting guidelines show the default plainly. One Texas insurer’s rules state that the default roof age equals the dwelling’s age, and that a newer date requires proof of a full replacement — overlays and partial replacements don’t count.

Why “Unknown” Costs You

  • Premium: an older assumed roof is rated as a higher risk, and you lose new-roof or impact-resistance credits.
  • Claim payout: if the policy pays roofs on a depreciation schedule or at actual cash value, the age on file drives the percentage you’re paid. See how roof age changes coverage.
  • Eligibility: an assumed age past 15 or 20 years can trigger an inspection request, an ACV endorsement, or a non-renewal.

Documents That Prove Roof Age

Insurers generally accept any dated document showing a full replacement. In rough order of strength:

  1. Paid, dated invoice or contract from the roofing contractor, showing a full tear-off and replacement and the property address.
  2. Building permit and final inspection. Search your city or county permit portal by address; many go back 15–20 years. Print the record showing the reroof permit and its final approval date.
  3. Manufacturer warranty registration. Shingle makers record the installation date when a warranty is registered.
  4. Seller’s disclosure and home inspection report from your purchase, if they state the year the roof was replaced.
  5. Roofer’s written opinion of age and condition — useful when no paperwork exists. Ask for remaining useful life to be stated as well.
  6. HOA or condo association records for communities that replace roofs on a schedule.
  7. Historical aerial imagery. Free historical imagery tools can show the year the roof’s color or pattern changed. It’s supporting evidence rather than proof on its own, but it can bracket the date.

If the previous owner replaced the roof, ask them (or their agent) for the invoice or the roofer’s name; the roofer may still have the job file.

How to Get the Date Corrected

  1. Ask your agent what roof year is on file and what source it came from.
  2. Send the proof in writing and ask for the policy to be re-rated from the date it’s accepted. Ask whether the change also removes an ACV roof endorsement or restores replacement cost.
  3. Check the next declarations page to confirm the change stuck. Corrected data doesn’t always flow through to renewal.
  4. After a new roof, report it right away. Forum threads are full of owners who paid for a new roof and kept paying old-roof premiums because nobody told the insurer. In Florida, a wind mitigation inspection on form OIR-B1-1802 can document roof-deck attachment and roof-to-wall connections for additional discounts.

If Aerial Imagery Triggered a Non-Renewal

Insurers increasingly make renewal decisions from imagery without sending anyone to the house. If a notice cites roof condition or age you believe is wrong:

  • Ask for the specific reason in writing, and the image or report it relied on.
  • Respond with dated photos of the roof and a written inspection by a licensed roofer or home inspector. Homeowners have reversed decisions this way, especially when the image showed a problem that had already been repaired.
  • Know your state’s rules. States set minimum notice periods for non-renewal, and Florida limits refusals based solely on the age of a roof under 15 years or one shown to have five years of remaining life. A state insurance department complaint is an option if an insurer won’t engage.
  • Shop early. If the decision stands, an independent agent can quote insurers with different roof guidelines before your coverage ends.

Buying a Home With an Unknown Roof Age

Get the roof date before your inspection contingency expires: ask the seller for the invoice or permit, check the permit portal yourself, and get a homeowners quote on the actual property before closing. Lenders require insurance at closing, and an unexpected ACV roof endorsement or a declined application is far easier to solve as a price negotiation than in the last week before closing. In Florida, insurers commonly require a four-point inspection on older homes, which reports roof condition and remaining life.

Frequently Asked Questions

What roof age do insurers use if it's unknown?

Many insurers default to the year the house was built. Published underwriting guidelines from at least one insurer state that the default roof age equals the dwelling's age unless the homeowner proves a full replacement. Insurers also use permit records, aerial imagery and inspections.

How do I prove the age of my roof for insurance?

Send a paid, dated invoice from the roofing contractor showing a full replacement, the building permit and final inspection record, a manufacturer warranty registration, a seller's disclosure or home inspection report stating the replacement year, or a roofer's written opinion of age and condition.

How do I find out when my roof was replaced?

Search your city or county building permit portal by address for a reroof permit, ask the previous owner or their real estate agent for the invoice or roofer's name, check the seller's disclosure and your purchase inspection report, or compare historical aerial imagery to see when the roof changed.

Should I tell my insurance company I got a new roof?

Yes. Send the dated invoice and permit and ask for the policy to be re-rated. A newer roof age can lower the premium, remove actual cash value roof terms, and qualify for roof or wind-mitigation discounts.

Can an insurer non-renew my policy based on aerial photos?

Insurers do use aerial and satellite imagery in renewal decisions. If you believe the image is wrong or outdated, ask for the reason and evidence in writing and respond with dated photos and a professional roof inspection. State notice rules and, in Florida, roof-age limits on non-renewal may also apply.
