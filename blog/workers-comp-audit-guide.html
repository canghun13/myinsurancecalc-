Workers' Comp Audit: How to Prepare and Avoid Overpaying

8 min read · Updated July 2026

Every workers' comp policy ends with an audit — a comparison of what you actually paid in payroll against the estimate your premium was based on. It's routine, not a sign you did something wrong, and it happens whether or not you filed a single claim all year. But routine doesn't mean risk-free: the same handful of paperwork gaps show up year after year, and they're responsible for the large, unexpected retroactive bills that catch small business owners off guard. Here's exactly what gets checked, where the money actually gets lost, and how to walk into the audit with nothing to worry about.

What the Audit Actually Compares

At the start of your policy, your premium was calculated using estimated payroll, projected employee classifications, and your experience modification rate (EMR). At audit, your carrier replaces those estimates with your actual numbers for the period. The simplified version of the formula is:

Annual Premium ≈ (Payroll ÷ 100) × Class Code Rate × Experience Modification

If your actual payroll came in higher than estimated, you owe additional premium. If it came in lower, you get a credit or refund. If a worker's actual job duties put them in a different classification than what you originally reported, that can shift the rate applied to their payroll too — sometimes significantly, since class code rates can vary by a factor of 10 or more between low-risk and high-risk classifications for the same business.

The Overtime Mistake That Costs the Most

In most states, only the straight-time portion of overtime pay counts toward your workers' comp payroll — the extra premium portion of "time-and-a-half" or "double time" is excluded. For example, if an employee's regular rate is $24/hour and they work overtime hours at $36/hour, only $24 of that overtime rate counts toward your payroll base; the additional $12/hour is excluded from the calculation. Businesses that don't separate straight-time from premium-time in their payroll records often end up reporting (and getting billed for) more payroll than they actually owe premium on. Keeping overtime hours tracked separately from regular hours, by employee, is one of the simplest ways to avoid overpaying.

The Subcontractor Mistake That Causes the Biggest Bills

This is where the largest surprise bills come from. If you paid a subcontractor or 1099 worker during the policy period and can't produce a valid certificate of insurance (COI) showing they carried their own workers' comp coverage for the dates they worked, your auditor can treat those payments as uninsured payroll exposure — adding the full amount to your own payroll base, even though that person was never your employee. A business that regularly uses subcontractors without collecting COIs can end up with a retroactive bill many times larger than its original estimated premium, simply because none of that subcontractor labor was accounted for at the start of the policy.

The fix is straightforward but has to happen before the audit, not after: collect a certificate of insurance from every subcontractor before they start work, confirm it specifically shows workers' compensation coverage (not just general liability, which is a common mix-up), and file it by service date so it lines up with your policy period when the audit happens.

Owner and Officer Exemption Certificates

Many states allow business owners, partners, or corporate officers to exempt themselves from workers' comp coverage (and the associated premium) by filing the required exemption certificate with the state. Without that certificate on file at audit time, an auditor may default to including owner or officer compensation in your payroll base — adding premium cost for coverage the owner may not have even wanted or needed. If you're eligible to exempt yourself, confirm the paperwork is filed correctly and kept current, since exemption rules and renewal requirements vary by state.

What to Have Ready Before the Audit

  • Payroll records by employee, with regular and overtime hours clearly separated.
  • Job classifications for each role, matching what employees actually do day-to-day rather than just a job title.
  • Certificates of insurance for every subcontractor used during the policy period, filed by service date.
  • Owner/officer exemption certificates, if applicable in your state.
  • General ledger or profit-and-loss statement, to cross-reference payments to contracted labor against your payroll records.

Don't Skip the Audit

Ignoring an audit request or refusing to cooperate is by far the most expensive mistake available. Carriers can apply a noncompliance charge of up to double your estimated premium, and they can cancel your policy outright — a canceled policy then makes new coverage harder and more expensive to find, since every insurer asks about prior cancellations on new applications. Responding promptly with organized documentation is always the cheaper path, even if you're worried the audit will turn up an unfavorable adjustment.

How This Connects to Your Experience Mod

Your audited payroll and classification data doesn't just settle this year's premium — accurate reporting also feeds into your experience modification rate (EMR) calculation for the following years, since that's built from your actual payroll and losses over a rolling three-year window. Clean, accurate audit results support an accurate EMR, while sloppy records can distort it in either direction. Bottom Line

A workers' comp audit isn't something to fear — it's a predictable, annual reconciliation, and the businesses that get surprised by it are almost always the ones with gaps in payroll documentation rather than the ones actually trying to underpay. Track overtime correctly, collect subcontractor certificates of insurance before work starts (not after a bill arrives), file owner/officer exemptions if you're eligible, and keep your records organized year-round. Do that, and the audit becomes a formality instead of a financial surprise.
