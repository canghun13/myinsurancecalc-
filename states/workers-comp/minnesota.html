Workers Compensation Requirements in Minnesota

Minnesota requires workers' compensation for all employers with one or more employees. The Department of Labor and Industry's Workers' Compensation Division oversees the system. Minnesota has a relatively well-functioning system with moderate claim costs and reasonable dispute resolution processes.

Workers Comp Rates in Minnesota

The average rate of approximately $1.78 per $100 of payroll is a statewide blended average — actual rates vary significantly by job classification.

For example, an employer with $1 million in payroll at the average rate of $1.78 per $100 would pay approximately $17,800 annually before experience modification adjustments.

What Makes Minnesota Unique

Minnesota has a system of attorney fees in workers' comp that are paid by the insurer rather than coming from the worker's benefit — a structure that encourages attorney involvement but also ensures workers aren't penalized for pursuing legitimate claims. This approach is different from many states and reflects Minnesota's worker-protection orientation.

Who Sets Workers’ Comp Rates in Minnesota

Minnesota doesn’t use NCCI for ratemaking. Rates and classifications come from the Minnesota Workers’ Compensation Insurers Association (MWCIA), an independent rating bureau, and Minnesota’s class codes and rules can differ from those used in NCCI states. Employers operating in several states should expect a Minnesota class code that doesn’t match the code on their other policies, and should check it at every audit.

How Much Workers’ Comp Pays in Minnesota (2026)

For a worker who can’t work at all while recovering, Minnesota workers’ comp replaces part of lost wages with a weekly check, separate from medical bills, which are paid in full by the insurer with no deductible. The key numbers:

Benefit Minnesota
Weekly wage-loss benefit (temporary total disability)66⅔% of your average weekly wage
Maximum benefit$1,594.08 a week for injuries on or after October 1, 2026 (Social Security Administration chart of state maximums)
Waiting periodNo wage-loss benefit for the first 3 days of disability
Waiting period paid back?Yes — if you’re out more than about 10 days, the waiting days are paid retroactively

A worker averaging $900 a week before the injury would receive about $600 a week. Because the check is two-thirds of wages up to the maximum, anyone who earned more than about $2,391 a week receives the maximum instead.

Checks change when your situation does: if you return to light duty at lower pay, temporary total disability usually switches to a partial benefit based on the wage difference, and once a doctor finds you’ve reached maximum medical improvement, any permanent impairment is paid under a separate permanent-disability schedule. The Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry adjusts the maximum periodically and resolves disputes over the weekly rate. If you’re an employer, the premium side of Minnesota workers’ comp is covered above; for how a lost-time claim moves your premium, see our EMR calculator.

Employee Rights in Minnesota

Minnesota workers may choose their own treating physician. Disputes are resolved through compensation judges in the Department of Labor and Industry with appeal to the Workers' Compensation Court of Appeals.

How to Reduce Workers Comp Costs in Minnesota

Minnesota employers benefit from a relatively functional system. Preventing musculoskeletal injuries (the most common claim category) through ergonomics and lifting programs is a top priority. Return-to-work programs reduce temporary disability claim duration. Minnesota's competitive market rewards safety performance through experience modification.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is workers' comp insurance required in Minnesota?

Yes. Minnesota requires workers' compensation coverage for employers with 1 or more employees. Coverage must be purchased from a private insurer or arranged through qualified self-insurance — Minnesota does not operate a competitive state fund, so employers compare quotes among private carriers only.

How much does workers' comp cost in Minnesota?

The statewide blended average in Minnesota is approximately $1.78 per $100 of payroll, though actual rates vary significantly by job classification and claims history — high-risk classifications like construction and roofing pay substantially more than clerical or retail classifications.

Who regulates workers' comp in Minnesota?

The Minnesota Department of Labor and Industry (DLI), through its Workers' Compensation Division, oversees Minnesota's workers' compensation system, handling claims administration, compliance, and dispute resolution between employees, employers, and insurers.

What happens if a Minnesota employer doesn't carry required workers' comp coverage?

Operating without required coverage exposes a Minnesota employer to civil penalties, potential personal liability for injury costs, and loss of the exclusive remedy protection that normally shields compliant employers from direct lawsuits over workplace injuries.

Does Minnesota use NCCI for workers’ comp?

No. Minnesota rates and classifications come from the Minnesota Workers' Compensation Insurers Association (MWCIA), an independent rating bureau.

How much does workers’ comp pay in Minnesota?

Minnesota workers' comp pays 66⅔% of your average weekly wage for temporary total disability, up to $1,594.08 a week for injuries on or after October 1, 2026. There is a waiting period of 3 days, paid back if you are out more than about 10 days. Medical treatment for the injury is covered in full.
