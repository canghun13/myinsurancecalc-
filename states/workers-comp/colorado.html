Workers Compensation Requirements in Colorado

Colorado requires workers' compensation for virtually all employers. Pinnacol Assurance is Colorado's state-chartered workers' comp insurer — not a true state fund but a quasi-governmental insurer that competes with private carriers. Colorado has a Division of Workers' Compensation within the Department of Labor and Employment.

Who Must Carry Workers’ Comp in Colorado (and Who Is Exempt)

Colorado has no small-employer exemption: a business with a single employee must carry workers’ comp, and anyone you pay for work is presumed to be an employee unless you can show otherwise. Payment doesn’t have to be wages — room and board or traded services count. The main categories:

Who Colorado rule
Employers with one or more employeesRequired — full-time, part-time, seasonal and family members all count
Corporate officers and LLC membersCovered as employees, but may reject coverage if they own at least 10% and are an officer (president, vice president, secretary, treasurer or board chair) or LLC member, by filing a rejection-of-coverage form with the Division
Sole proprietors and partnersNot employees; not required to cover themselves (can elect coverage)
Domestic workersExempt if they work fewer than 40 hours and fewer than five days a week for you
Casual maintenance, repair, farm and ranch workersExempt if paid less than $2,000 in a calendar year
Commission-only real estate agents, volunteers, rideshare drivers for transportation network companiesExempt

Independent contractors: issuing a 1099 doesn’t settle it. Under the Workers’ Compensation Act a contractor must be free from your control and direction and customarily engaged in an independent business doing that kind of work; if you control when, where and how the work is done, expect the person to be treated as your employee. Subcontractors without their own coverage can become the general contractor’s responsibility — collect certificates (see verifying subcontractor insurance).

Penalties: under C.R.S. 8-43-409, the Division can fine an employer that fails to insure up to $250 a day for an initial violation and $250 to $500 a day for later violations, and can order the business to stop operating. Those fines come on top of the increased benefits an uninsured employer owes an injured worker under 8-43-408. Coverage must be continuous — a lapse counts. Employers must also post the Division’s workers’ comp notice where employees can see it.

Workers Comp Rates in Colorado

The average rate of approximately $1.68 per $100 of payroll is a statewide blended average — actual rates vary significantly by job classification.

For example, an employer with $1 million in payroll at the average rate of $1.68 per $100 would pay approximately $16,800 annually before experience modification adjustments.

What Makes Colorado Unique

Pinnacol Assurance is a unique entity — a quasi-governmental insurance company that is self-sustaining and not funded by taxpayers. It operates competitively in the workers' comp market while also serving as the insurer of last resort. Pinnacol has been noted for its customer service and claims management approach.

Who Sets Workers’ Comp Rates in Colorado

Colorado is an NCCI state. NCCI files advisory loss costs and class codes, and each insurer applies its own approved multiplier, plus schedule credits or debits, to arrive at your rate. That’s why quotes on identical payroll and class codes can differ meaningfully in Colorado, and why comparing carriers at renewal is worth the effort. Our blended estimate of about $1.68 per $100 of payroll hides an enormous range — clerical work is typically a small fraction of that, roofing many times more.

Where Pinnacol Assurance Fits in the Colorado Market

Pinnacol Assurance is Colorado’s former state fund, now a competitive public insurer that writes the largest share of the market and must accept any Colorado employer. If private carriers decline you, start there.

How Much Workers’ Comp Pays in Colorado (2026)

For a worker who can’t work at all while recovering, Colorado workers’ comp replaces part of lost wages with a weekly check, separate from medical bills, which are paid in full by the insurer with no deductible. The key numbers:

Benefit Colorado
Weekly wage-loss benefit (temporary total disability)66⅔% of your average weekly wage
Maximum benefit$1,448.02 a week for injuries on or after July 1, 2026 (Social Security Administration chart of state maximums)
Waiting periodNo wage-loss benefit for the first 3 work shifts of disability
Waiting period paid back?Yes — if you’re out more than about 14 days, the waiting days are paid retroactively

A worker averaging $900 a week before the injury would receive about $600 a week. Because the check is two-thirds of wages up to the maximum, anyone who earned more than about $2,172 a week receives the maximum instead.

Checks change when your situation does: if you return to light duty at lower pay, temporary total disability usually switches to a partial benefit based on the wage difference, and once a doctor finds you’ve reached maximum medical improvement, any permanent impairment is paid under a separate permanent-disability schedule. The Colorado Division of Workers’ Compensation adjusts the maximum periodically and resolves disputes over the weekly rate. If you’re an employer, the premium side of Colorado workers’ comp is covered above; for how a lost-time claim moves your premium, see our EMR calculator.

Owner and Officer Payroll Limits in Colorado

When owners are covered by a Colorado workers’ comp policy, insurers don’t simply use what the owner takes home. Rating rules set a minimum and maximum annual payroll for executive officers, and a set amount or cap for sole proprietors and partners, and the premium audit applies them.

Who Payroll used for premium
Corporate officers / LLC members (when covered)Flat $79,800
Sole proprietors and partners (if they elect coverage)Counted at a flat $79,800 a year
Effective for policies starting on or afterJanuary 1, 2026

Colorado uses a single flat amount: an included officer, owner or partner is counted at $79,800 regardless of actual pay.

Owners who are allowed to exclude themselves, and do, add no payroll at all — but they also have no coverage. Figures are from published 2026 carrier billing tables and change each year; the values in force on your policy’s effective date are the ones that apply at audit. Employee Rights in Colorado

Colorado workers have the right to choose their own physician after an initial authorized visit. The Division of Workers' Compensation handles disputes through an administrative process with access to appellate courts.

How to Reduce Workers Comp Costs in Colorado

Colorado employers benefit from Pinnacol's competitive pricing and service. Safety programs, experience modification management, and return-to-work programs reduce costs. Colorado's relatively moderate rates reflect a balanced system with reasonable litigation levels.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is workers' comp insurance required in Colorado?

Yes. Colorado requires workers' compensation coverage for employers with 1 or more employees. Colorado's state-chartered insurer, Pinnacol Assurance, competes directly with private carriers rather than acting as a monopolistic fund.

How much does workers' comp cost in Colorado?

The statewide blended average in Colorado is approximately $1.68 per $100 of payroll, though actual rates vary significantly by job classification and claims history — high-risk classifications like construction and roofing pay substantially more than clerical or retail classifications.

Who regulates workers' comp in Colorado?

the Colorado Division of Workers' Compensation, within the Department of Labor and Employment oversees Colorado's workers' compensation system, handling claims administration, compliance, and dispute resolution between employees, employers, and insurers.

What happens if a Colorado employer doesn't carry required workers' comp coverage?

Operating without required coverage exposes a Colorado employer to civil penalties, potential personal liability for injury costs, and loss of the exclusive remedy protection that normally shields compliant employers from direct lawsuits over workplace injuries.

Does shopping lower workers’ comp premiums in Colorado?

Usually, yes. Colorado is an NCCI loss cost state, so each insurer applies its own multiplier and schedule credits to the same loss costs, and quotes on identical payroll can differ.

What is Pinnacol Assurance?

Pinnacol Assurance is Colorado's former state fund, now a competitive public insurer that writes the largest share of the market and must accept any Colorado employer.

How much does workers’ comp pay in Colorado?

Colorado workers' comp pays 66⅔% of your average weekly wage for temporary total disability, up to $1,448.02 a week for injuries on or after July 1, 2026. There is a waiting period of 3 work shifts, paid back if you are out more than about 14 days. Medical treatment for the injury is covered in full.

What is the minimum payroll for an owner on a workers’ comp policy in Colorado?

Colorado counts a covered officer, owner or partner at a flat $79,800 a year for workers' comp premium, for policies effective on or after January 1, 2026.

Is workers’ comp required in Colorado for one employee?

Yes. Colorado requires workers' compensation for any employer with one or more employees, including part-time, seasonal and family workers. Sole proprietors and partners are not required to cover themselves, and corporate officers and LLC members who own at least 10% may reject coverage by filing with the Division of Workers' Compensation.

What is the penalty for not having workers’ comp in Colorado?

Under C.R.S. 8-43-409, up to $250 per day for an initial violation and $250 to $500 per day for second and later violations, plus a possible cease-and-desist order. An uninsured employer is also liable for increased benefits to an injured worker.
