Workers Compensation Requirements in Nevada

Nevada requires workers' compensation for all employers with one or more employees. Nevada no longer has a state fund: its former state industrial insurance system privatized in 1999 and became Employers Insurance Company of Nevada, now part of the private insurer EMPLOYERS. The Division of Industrial Relations oversees the system. Nevada's hospitality, construction, and gaming industries generate significant workers' comp activity.

Workers Comp Rates in Nevada

The average rate of approximately $1.82 per $100 of payroll is a statewide blended average — actual rates vary significantly by job classification.

For example, an employer with $1 million in payroll at the average rate of $1.82 per $100 would pay approximately $18,200 annually before experience modification adjustments.

What Makes Nevada Unique

Nevada's gaming and hospitality industries create unique workers' comp considerations — hotel and casino workers face repetitive motion injuries, slip-and-fall risks, and customer-related injuries that require specialized claims management. Las Vegas's 24-hour economy also means night shift and irregular schedule workers who may be more vulnerable to fatigue-related injuries.

Who Sets Workers’ Comp Rates in Nevada

Nevada is an NCCI state. NCCI files advisory loss costs and class codes, and each insurer applies its own approved multiplier, plus schedule credits or debits, to arrive at your rate. That’s why quotes on identical payroll and class codes can differ meaningfully in Nevada, and why comparing carriers at renewal is worth the effort. Our blended estimate of about $1.82 per $100 of payroll hides an enormous range — clerical work is typically a small fraction of that, roofing many times more.

Where EMPLOYERS Fits in the Nevada Market

EMPLOYERS is descended from Nevada’s former state industrial insurance system, which privatized in 1999 — Nevada no longer has a state fund. It competes with private carriers, so get its quote alongside at least one other insurer rather than treating either as the default.

Nevada’s Maximum Weekly Benefit

Injured workers in Nevada receive wage-loss benefits based on their average weekly wage, up to a statutory cap. The maximum weekly benefit is $1,308.86, effective July 1, 2026 (Social Security Administration chart of state maximums). The cap matters for employers with well-paid staff: a claim by a high earner is paid at the maximum, so lost-time claims from skilled trades and professionals cost more than claims from lower-wage roles — one reason the experience modifier responds so strongly to a single serious claim.

Workers Comp Benefits in Nevada

Nevada provides temporary total disability at 66.67% of average weekly wage. Medical benefits cover necessary treatment through authorized providers. Permanent partial disability uses impairment ratings.

Employee Rights in Nevada

Nevada workers select their own treating physician from a provider panel approved by the employer. The Division of Industrial Relations provides hearing officers for dispute resolution.

How to Reduce Workers Comp Costs in Nevada

Nevada hospitality and gaming employers should focus on ergonomics, safe patient/guest handling, and slip-trip-fall prevention as the primary injury categories. Las Vegas's large employer base means competitive workers' comp market with many insurer options. Return-to-work programs in Nevada's service economy require modified duty planning.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is workers' comp insurance required in Nevada?

Yes. Nevada requires workers' compensation coverage for all employers with one or more employees, with no exemption for sole proprietors who are the only worker in some other states — Nevada's rules are stricter and generally treat all workers, including many independent contractors, as covered employees.

How much does workers' comp cost in Nevada?

The statewide blended average is approximately $1.82 per $100 of payroll, though actual rates vary significantly by job classification — a clerical worker might pay $0.20-$0.40 per $100 while a roofing contractor might pay $15-$25 per $100.

Can Nevada businesses self-insure for workers' comp?

Yes, in addition to purchasing coverage from private insurers, qualifying employers can apply to self-insure through Nevada's Division of Industrial Relations, though this option is typically limited to larger, financially established businesses.

What happens if a Nevada employer doesn't carry workers' comp?

Operating without required workers' comp coverage in Nevada exposes an employer to significant penalties, potential personal liability for injury costs, and loss of the exclusive remedy protection that normally shields employers from direct lawsuits over workplace injuries.

Does shopping lower workers’ comp premiums in Nevada?

Usually, yes. Nevada is an NCCI loss cost state, so each insurer applies its own multiplier and schedule credits to the same loss costs, and quotes on identical payroll can differ.

What is the maximum weekly workers’ comp benefit in Nevada?

$1,308.86, effective July 1, 2026.
