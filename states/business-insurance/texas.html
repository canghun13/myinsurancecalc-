What Makes Texas Business Insurance Unique

Texas is the only state where workers' comp remains fully optional for most private employers — but opting out ('non-subscriber' status) removes the legal protections the system provides and exposes the business to direct injury lawsuits with no damage caps.

The Real Texas Question: Subscribe or Not?

Texas is the only state where most private employers can legally choose not to carry workers’ comp. That makes the Texas small business insurance decision different from every other state: instead of asking “how much is the required coverage,” a Texas owner is comparing two different risk positions.

Opting out removes the premium, not the risk. A non-subscriber that loses the three common-law defenses can be found liable even when the injured employee was partly at fault, and juries decide the damages. When Texas Effectively Requires Coverage Anyway

“Optional” has important exceptions that catch businesses moving into new kinds of work:

  • Government construction. Under Texas Labor Code §406.096, a governmental entity entering a building or construction contract must require the contractor to certify that it provides workers’ comp coverage for each employee on the project — and the requirement flows down to subcontractors. A non-subscriber cannot work on public building projects.
  • Private contracts. Many general contractors, property managers, and corporate clients require a certificate showing workers’ comp before a vendor is allowed on site. For a non-subscriber, this is a contract requirement, not a legal one, but the effect is the same.
  • Out-of-state work. Texas’s opt-out does not travel. Employees working in another state may trigger that state’s mandatory coverage.

A business that plans to bid public work or grow into larger commercial clients usually ends up subscribing; deciding early avoids rebuilding insurance mid-contract.

Occupational Injury Benefit Plans Are Not Workers’ Comp

Many Texas non-subscribers buy a private occupational injury benefit plan (sometimes sold with employer’s liability coverage) to pay medical and wage benefits to injured employees. These plans can be useful, but they do not restore the common-law defenses a non-subscriber gives up, and they are not a certificate of workers’ comp for contracts that require one. Compare the employer’s liability limit in such a plan with what a serious lawsuit could cost, not with the price of a workers’ comp policy.

Coastal Wind and Hail: The Texas Property Problem

Texas commercial property pricing is driven by weather more than by any statute. Two issues dominate:

  • Coastal windstorm. In the 14 first-tier coastal counties and part of Harris County, many standard property policies exclude wind and hail. Wind coverage there is commonly placed with the Texas Windstorm Insurance Association (TWIA), the state-created residual insurer, alongside a property policy that excludes wind. A Gulf Coast business needs to confirm wind is covered somewhere — it is easy to buy a BOP that leaves it out.
  • Hail inland. Across North and Central Texas, hail is the most frequent property loss, and many policies carry a separate wind/hail deductible, often set as a percentage of the building’s insured value rather than a flat amount. Compare that deductible before comparing premiums.

Flood is excluded from standard property policies everywhere, including Texas, and requires separate coverage.

Commercial Auto and Liability in Texas

Texas is an at-fault auto state with minimum liability limits of $30,000 per person, $60,000 per accident for bodily injury, and $25,000 for property damage. Those minimums are rarely enough for a business vehicle: a single serious accident involving a company truck can exceed them many times over, and client contracts typically require $1 million combined single limit. Employees using their own cars for errands leave a gap that a personal policy does not fill — see hired and non-owned auto coverage.

General liability is not legally required for most Texas businesses, but certain licensed trades and municipal permits impose their own minimums, and commercial leases almost always do.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is workers' comp insurance required in Texas?

No — Texas is the only state where workers' comp is optional (opt-in) for most private employers. Opting out ("non-subscriber" status) means losing standard legal defenses against injury lawsuits, so many Texas employers carry coverage voluntarily despite it not being legally required.

How much does small business insurance cost in Texas?

A standard Business Owner's Policy (BOP) bundling general liability and commercial property coverage costs an estimated $780 per year for a small business in Texas, based on industry average data. Actual premiums vary by industry, payroll size, and claims history.

Is general liability insurance required in Texas?

No, general liability insurance isn't legally mandated in Texas for most businesses. It's frequently required contractually, though — commercial landlords, client contracts, and certain licensed professions often require proof of coverage.

Do LLC owners need workers' comp in Texas?

It depends on management role. LLC managers who can sign contracts or make business decisions are typically excluded from the requirement, while non-management members working for the LLC generally must be covered. This exemption is usually capped, so larger LLCs should confirm their specific obligation.

Is workers’ comp required for small businesses in Texas?

Not for most private employers. Texas is the only state where private employers can choose not to carry workers’ comp. Non-subscribers lose key legal defenses if an employee sues over a work injury, must still file Form DWC-005 annually and post notices, and generally cannot work on public building or construction contracts, which require coverage under Texas Labor Code §406.096.

What is the downside of being a non-subscriber in Texas?

An injured employee can sue the business directly, and a non-subscriber cannot use contributory negligence, assumption of risk, or the fellow-employee rule as defenses. Damages are decided by a court rather than limited to workers’ comp benefits. Many contracts also require proof of workers’ comp before a vendor can work on site.

Does an occupational injury plan replace workers’ comp in Texas?

No. A private occupational injury benefit plan can pay medical and wage benefits to injured employees, but it does not restore the legal defenses a non-subscriber gives up and does not satisfy contracts that require a workers’ comp certificate.

Does a Texas business owner’s policy cover wind and hail?

Inland policies often cover wind and hail subject to a separate deductible, frequently set as a percentage of the insured building value. In the first-tier coastal counties and part of Harris County, many standard policies exclude wind, and coverage is commonly placed with the Texas Windstorm Insurance Association. Flood is always a separate policy.

What are the minimum auto insurance limits for a business vehicle in Texas?

Texas requires at least $30,000 per person and $60,000 per accident for bodily injury and $25,000 for property damage. These are legal minimums only; business contracts commonly require $1 million combined single limit, and the minimums are rarely adequate for a commercial vehicle.
